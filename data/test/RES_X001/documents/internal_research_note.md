1. This note provides general background in response to a query from Foxley Capital LLP (incorporated in England and Wales, company number 15689892, registered office at 136 Bishopsgate, London) concerning the distinction between representations and warranties under English law, and the significance of that distinction to a buyer in a transaction context.

2. In commercial contracts, particularly in share or asset purchase agreements governed by English law, the terms "representation" and "warranty" are often used interchangeably but carry distinct legal consequences. A representation is a statement of fact made by one party before the contract is formed, which induces the other party to enter into the agreement. If the statement is false, it may give rise to a claim for misrepresentation under the Misrepresentation Act 1967. A warranty, by contrast, is a contractual term that, if breached, entitles the injured party to damages for breach of contract.

3. The legal remedy available to a buyer depends on whether a false statement is classified as a representation or a warranty. If a representation is found to be untrue, the buyer may, depending on the nature of the misrepresentation (fraudulent, negligent, or innocent), have the right to rescind the contract and/or claim damages. However, rights to rescission may be lost if affirmed or time has elapsed. Where a warranty is breached, the buyer is generally limited to a claim for damages, but the contractual terms may specify remedies such as price adjustments or indemnities.

4. It matters to the buyer because the classification affects both the scope of available remedies and the applicable limitation periods. Claims for misrepresentation under English law are subject to specific statutory limitation rules under section 2(2) of the Limitation Act 1980, which provides a six-year long-stop from the date of contract, but allows courts discretion to extend time in cases of fraud, concealment, or mistake. In contrast, contractual claims for breach of warranty are also subject to a six-year limitation period under section 5 of the Limitation Act 1980, running from breach (typically the date of completion).

5. Additionally, parties often reclassify representations as contractual warranties in the agreement to avoid the complexities of misrepresentation claims and to cap or limit liability, particularly where Fenwick Energy plc (incorporated in England and Wales, company number 13985008, registered office at 39 Cheapside, London) is a material customer in a transactional context. This allows for greater predictability and control over risk allocation in negotiated deals governed by English law.
